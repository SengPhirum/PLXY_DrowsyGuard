02/08
The problem

Why one frame is not enough

A closed eye is not drowsiness.
A closed eye for long enough is.

  • 01

    Single-frame alarms cry wolf

    Normal blinking, looking down at the gear stick, glasses glare and a moment of lost face all read as “eyes closed” to a per-frame classifier.

  • 02

    Appearance is not eyelid state

    Our own whole-face network scored 0.81 in validation but 0.57 on drivers it had never seen — it had learned who the driver was, not whether their eyes were shut. It was removed.

  • 03

    Cambodia's older fleet has no monitoring

    Driver monitoring ships only in new vehicles. A USD 27 retrofit that runs offline is the only version of this that reaches an existing car.

03/08
Concept — cues

Four signs, each with its own clock

What the camera measures

PERCLOS3.0 s

Share of the last three seconds with the eyes closed. The best-established fatigue measure, so it carries most of the weight.

weight 0.55
Long blink>0.40 s

Slower than a blink, shorter than a microsleep. Blink speed drops before a driver knows they are tired.

weight 0.20
Yawn≥1.20 s

Mouth corners drop and pull inward. Anything faster is speech, not a yawn.

weight 0.15
Head nod0.3–1.5 s

Down and back. Two independent pitch channels must agree, so an open mouth cannot fake it.

weight 0.10
04/08
Concept — decision

From four numbers to one spoken word

Risk has to be held, not touched

  • 01

    Fuse, weighted

    risk = 0.55 PERCLOS + 0.20 blink + 0.15 yawn + 0.10 nod. The three minor cues corroborate; none of them can raise an alarm alone.

  • 02

    Hold, then fire

    Risk must stay above 0.55 for 0.55 s. A single noisy frame decrements the streak instead of resetting it, so one dropout inside a real closure cannot end it.

  • 03

    Name the cause out loud

    “Drowsy”, “Microsleep”, “Yawning”, “HeadNod” — a named warning is actionable in a way a chime is not. Three announcements per episode, then quiet.

  • 04

    Wi-Fi is never load-bearing

    The alert path never touches the network. A dropped dashboard costs diagnostics, never safety.

What running it on hardware taught us: the sensor is mounted upside down, so every frame arrives mirrored. That reversed the eye pair and flipped the sign of every vertical measurement — jaw drop read −1.2 instead of +1.2 and fell as the mouth opened, so the yawn cue had never once been able to fire. No amount of desk testing would have found it.

08/08
Conclusion

Where the remaining effort goes

The hardware is done arguing.
The next win is in the model.

Our focus from here is training the lightweight on-device model — 11,250 parameters, 46 KB — on visible-light eye-state data, split by subject, so it reads eyelids instead of faces.

  • →

    Fine-tune in the target domain

    Infrared-trained, it does not transfer to 45 px visible-light eye crops: AUC 0.62. Grayscale, CLAHE, inversion and four patch scales did not recover it. The fix is labelled data in our own domain.

  • →

    Judge it per driver, never on average

    Subject-independent splits only. The 0.81-to-0.57 collapse is the standard any replacement has to clear.

  • →

    Then tune the thresholds on real video

    Every period on slide 3 is literature-informed and tested on synthetic traces. Labelled yawn and nod footage turns them from defaults into measurements.

Why not a large model: the most accurate public drowsiness networks are 70–343 MB at 224×224 and cannot run on an ESP32‑S3 at any frame rate. The eye-closure route is the one that fits. Research prototype only — not a certified safety device.
